03HubAgreed core

Your community. The game itself.

The Hub is a community builder. You choose one region of the world and it becomes yours. Its climate, food, buildings and people follow the real place it's modelled on.

You build it up, get to know the people who live there, and keep it standing when a crisis hits. Everything else in the game feeds it. Think Clash of Clans: people play for the base, not the side modes.

One session, about 10 minutes
01Check in. See what changed since yesterday.
02Build or fix one thing. Every build helps one goal and costs another.
03Talk to someone. Residents tell you what the numbers can't.
04A crisis lands. Make the call, then see what really happened.
05Ask for help, or send it. Friends' regions are on your map.

AI residents

In my words / James

I'm genuinely excited about this one. Very few games have live, conversational characters baked in yet, and chatbots are now convincing enough to pass for human in Turing-test studies. We're early, and that's the opportunity.

Here's the value prop. As an American, I can't fully feel what's happening on the Gaza Strip. But if a character in a game I'm sucked into tells me how they feel, what they've lost and what they need, I'm a lot more likely to feel it. Then I'm working to help them, and maybe rallying my friends to help too.

It also makes a single-player world feel multiplayer. Your avatar walks around talking to dozens of people who remember you, and they show up for you when things go wrong.

Example / Amara, clinic nurse
The river's up again. If it floods, the clinic loses power first.
Can we move the generator upstairs tonight?
Tomas has a truck. He trusts you after the well fix. Want me to ask him?
01A persona card per resident, written by the team. The AI stays inside it.
02Memory and trust. Promises kept or broken change how they treat you.
03Off screen, they keep living. Choices ripple over time.
04Guardrails: a small, low-energy model, clearly fictional characters, no unhealthy attachment, screened crisis talk.

04Mini gamesConfirmed Nov 9

Levels that climb what people need.

The LabTabled Nov 9 / full write-up

A sandbox for wicked problems: problems with no single right answer, where fixing one part shifts the others. Players stake resources on a brief, and the best work goes to real partners. Tabled on Nov 9: not enough research or bandwidth yet.

Two tracks

Track A / Science

Partner puzzles

A research partner hands us a task where human eyes beat computers: spotting patterns, mapping, sorting. We wrap it in play, and many players solve each piece so answers can be cross-checked.

Track B / Policy

Wicked-problem sandbox

Players design solutions under real limits, with one side building the plan and another playing what goes wrong. We group the strongest approaches and hand them to NGO partners. Less precedent here. This is where we'd be breaking ground.

It has worked before

2011 / Foldit

Players solved a viral enzyme

Protein-folding players produced models good enough to solve a retroviral protease structure, opening routes to new antiretroviral drugs.

Nature Reviews Microbiology
2012 / Cell Slider

200,000 people read cancer slides

Cancer Research UK's players classified nearly 2 million images, cutting analysis from 18 months to 3.

European Medical Journal
2014 / Genes in Space

Flying through cancer data

A spaceship game where each route players flew helped scientists find faulty genes in breast cancer data.

Cancer Research UK
2016 / Sea Hero Quest

4.3 million players, dementia

Navigation data built the first benchmark of human spatial navigation, a lead toward earlier dementia diagnosis.

Deutsche Telekom
2024 / Borderlands Science

Inside a hit commercial game

4.5 million Borderlands 3 players mapped evolutionary links between gut microbes, published in Nature Biotechnology.

ScienceDaily
What they share

The pattern we copy

A partner owns the question. Humans beat the computer at the task. Play stays fun. Many players check each answer. Players get credit.

The wager

01Accept a brief and stake resources from your Hub.
02Build within set tools, budget and time.
03Survive the consequences round.
04Pass and earn a multiple of your stake, top-player status, and a shot at the Hall. Fail and lose the stake.
Playbook / how game teams actually work

Playbook

We're a game company now, so we build like one. This is the process studios use, cut down to fit the three and a half weeks we have before Dec 3.

01The rules studios follow

Find the fun before you build the game.

Every source below says the same thing in a different way. Prove the core is worth playing while it's still cheap to change. Then build.

Sony / Mark Cerny's Method

Two projects, back to back

Pre-production is for discovery and can't be scheduled like a build. It ends with a first playable and a big-picture design. If the first playable isn't good, production won't rescue it.

For us: this week is discovery. Building starts once we have something small that plays.

Supercell

Small team, gameplay first

Tiny teams own their game, put gameplay ahead of everything else, and drop what isn't working early instead of late.

For us: eight people is the right size. We cut features without drama.

Epic Games / Fortnite

Build on one foundation

Battle Royale took about two months, but only because it sat on top of a game Epic had been building since 2011. The mode was fast because the base existed.

For us: the Hub is the base. Build it once, then the other modes are cheap.

Design pillars

Three or four fixed feelings

Teams agree on a few pillars: the feelings the game must deliver. Every new idea has to pass through them. The test is "does it serve this game", not "is it a good idea".

For us: four proposed pillars below. They end idea debates fast.

Core loop

The thing you repeat

The core loop is the short cycle of actions a player repeats. Studios prototype it first, in ugly boxes or on paper, before spending anything on art.

For us: build, crisis, decide, consequence. Test that before anything else.

Playtesting

Early, small, specific

Put a rough build in front of a few outsiders, watch what they do, and ask one question: did you want to play again?

For us: three outsiders play the grey build on Nov 19. Mark runs it.

On Epic: the two-month figure is well documented. Epic's internal idea process is not public in any detail, so nothing here claims to copy it.
