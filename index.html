03HubAgreed core

Your region. The game itself.

You're the Steward of one city. You live in it: you walk the streets, know the residents and build relationships with them. You also make the city-level calls about what gets built and how the place responds.

The city starts as its real-world twin, renamed: same climate, buildings, weak spots and problems. Crises come two ways: real ones when real events happen, and simulated ones every week or two. Think Clash of Clans: people play for the base. Other players can run their own copy of the same city, and the Hub is where everyone's cities meet.

One session, about 10 minutes
01Check in. See what changed since yesterday.
02Build or fix one thing. Every build helps one goal and costs another.
03Talk to someone. Residents tell you what the numbers can't.
04A crisis lands. Make the call, then see what really happened.
05Ask for help, or send it. Friends' regions are on your map.

AI residents

In my words / James

I'm genuinely excited about this one. Very few games have live, conversational characters baked in yet, and chatbots are now convincing enough to pass for human in Turing-test studies. We're early, and that's the opportunity.

Here's the value prop. As an American, I can't fully feel what's happening on the Gaza Strip. But if a character in a game I'm sucked into tells me how they feel, what they've lost and what they need, I'm a lot more likely to feel it. Then I'm working to help them, and maybe rallying my friends to help too.

It also makes a single-player world feel multiplayer. Your avatar walks around talking to dozens of people who remember you, and they show up for you when things go wrong.

The LabTabled Nov 9 / full write-up

A sandbox for wicked problems: problems with no single right answer, where fixing one part shifts the others. Players stake resources on a brief, and the best work goes to real partners. Tabled on Nov 9: not enough research or bandwidth yet. Nov 12 added: players could upload solutions to a shared pool, and the best go into the Hub.

Two tracks

Track A / Science

Partner puzzles

A research partner hands us a task where human eyes beat computers: spotting patterns, mapping, sorting. We wrap it in play, and many players solve each piece so answers can be cross-checked.

Track B / Policy

Wicked-problem sandbox

Players design solutions under real limits, with one side building the plan and another playing what goes wrong. We group the strongest approaches and hand them to NGO partners. Less precedent here. This is where we'd be breaking ground.

It has worked before

2011 / Foldit

Players solved a viral enzyme

Protein-folding players produced models good enough to solve a retroviral protease structure, opening routes to new antiretroviral drugs.

Nature Reviews Microbiology
2012 / Cell Slider

200,000 people read cancer slides

Cancer Research UK's players classified nearly 2 million images, cutting analysis from 18 months to 3.

European Medical Journal
2014 / Genes in Space

Flying through cancer data

A spaceship game where each route players flew helped scientists find faulty genes in breast cancer data.

Cancer Research UK
2016 / Sea Hero Quest

4.3 million players, dementia

Navigation data built the first benchmark of human spatial navigation, a lead toward earlier dementia diagnosis.

Deutsche Telekom
2024 / Borderlands Science

Inside a hit commercial game

4.5 million Borderlands 3 players mapped evolutionary links between gut microbes, published in Nature Biotechnology.

ScienceDaily
What they share

The pattern we copy

A partner owns the question. Humans beat the computer at the task. Play stays fun. Many players check each answer. Players get credit.

The wager

01Accept a brief and stake resources from your Hub.
02Build within set tools, budget and time.
03Survive the consequences round.
04Pass and earn a multiple of your stake, top-player status, and a shot at the Hall. Fail and lose the stake.
